<|det|>title [140, 468, 479, 494]<|/det|>1.1 Division Algorithm
<|det|>text [138, 511, 868, 569]<|/det|>Divisibility is a fundamental concept in number theory. Let \( a \) and \( d \) be integers. We say that \( d \) is a divisor of \( a \), and that \( a \) is a multiple of \( d \), if there exists an integer \( q \) such that
<|det|>equation [463, 578, 537, 597]<|/det|>\[
a = d q.
\]
<|det|>text [139, 605, 371, 623]<|/det|>If \(d\) divides \(a\), we write
<|det|>equation [480, 624, 521, 642]<|/det|>\[
d | a.
\]
<|det|>text [138, 647, 865, 684]<|/det|>For example, 1001 is divisible by 7 and 13. Divisibility is transitive: If \( a \) divides \( b \) and \( b \) divides \( c \), then \( a \) divides \( c \) (Exercise 14).
<|det|>text [138, 686, 877, 780]<|/det|>The minimum principle states that every nonempty set of integers bounded below contains a smallest element. For example, a nonempty set of nonnegative integers must contain a smallest element. We can see the necessity of the condition that the nonempty set be bounded below by considering the example of the set Z of all integers, positive, negative, and zero.
<|det|>text [138, 781, 865, 817]<|/det|>The minimum principle is all we need to prove the following important result.
<|det|>text [140, 829, 865, 848]<|/det|>Theorem 1.1 (Division algorithm) Let \( a \) and \( d \) be integers with \( d \geq 1 \).
<|det|>text [141, 849, 585, 868]<|/det|>There exist unique integers \(q\) and \(r\) such that
<|det|>equation [446, 876, 860, 896]<|/det|>\[
a = d q + r \tag {1.1}
\]
<|det|>text [141, 904, 186, 920]<|/det|>and
<|det|>equation [429, 922, 860, 942]<|/det|>\[
0 \leq r \leq d - 1. \tag {1.2}
\]
